# DUTYRA — Master Game Design and Development Notes

**Status date:** July 13, 2026
**Project:** DUTYRA
**Genre:** Realistic open-world police career simulator
**Engine:** Godot
**Repository:** `hoovflip1999/DUTYRA`

---

# 1. Game Overview

DUTYRA is intended to be a realistic police-career simulator built around one officer’s entire career.

The player does not select disconnected missions or switch between unrelated characters. The player creates one officer, joins a department, works patrol shifts, gains experience, earns qualifications, receives promotions, and lives with the long-term consequences of their decisions.

The game should feel like an active police world rather than a traditional mission-based police game.

The core experience is:

1. Begin a shift.
2. Become available for calls.
3. Patrol the open world.
4. Receive or encounter incidents.
5. Decide how to respond.
6. Investigate and interact.
7. Take enforcement action, provide assistance, or leave.
8. Document and clear the incident.
9. Experience career, financial, legal, physical, and world consequences.
10. End the shift and continue building the same officer’s career.

---

# 2. Core Game Philosophy

## Player choice creates world reaction

The main DUTYRA rule is:

> **The game should not force the player to make the correct decision. The world should react to the decision they make.**

The game may explain that an action lacks legal justification, violates policy, or creates danger, but it should generally not block the player from performing it.

Examples include:

* Conducting an unjustified search.
* Arresting someone without enough evidence.
* Leaving a crash without reporting it.
* Ignoring an injured person.
* Refusing medical treatment.
* Continuing a pursuit against policy.
* Using unnecessary force.
* Failing to complete a report.
* Leaving a call before it is resolved.

The game records what happened and reacts through systems such as:

* Witness statements.
* Body-camera footage.
* Dash-camera footage.
* Security cameras.
* Complaints.
* Supervisor reviews.
* Internal Affairs.
* Court cases.
* Civil lawsuits.
* Department discipline.
* Retraining.
* Suspension.
* Termination.
* Reputation changes.
* Financial consequences.
* NPC behavior.
* News coverage.
* Career delays.

## No obvious “correct answer” system

DUTYRA should not constantly display:

* Correct choice.
* Wrong choice.
* Good cop points.
* Bad cop points.
* Public trust minus 10.
* Morality meter.
* Mission failed.

Consequences should usually appear through believable parts of the world instead.

## Relevant choices are easy; unusual choices remain possible

The interaction design rule is:

> **Show the player the most relevant choices immediately while always leaving a path to broader actions.**

The game should not display every possible action at once.

Common actions should be easy to reach. Rare, unusual, or questionable actions can appear one level deeper.

## The game simulates circumstances, not scripts

Calls provide starting circumstances, but they should not completely control what the player can do.

A noise complaint should not become a rigid sequence of:

1. Talk to resident.
2. Give warning.
3. Leave.

Instead, the player may:

* Speak calmly.
* Demand identification.
* Separate people.
* Investigate another possible crime.
* Request backup.
* Issue a citation.
* Arrest someone.
* Ignore the complaint.
* Leave the area.
* Escalate unnecessarily.

The situation and world then respond.

# 3. World Structure

## One open world

The current design is for one main open world rather than separate game modes for every department.

The world should contain different departmental regions and jurisdictions, such as:

* City police areas.
* County sheriff areas.
* Highway patrol areas.
* Rural roads.
* Commercial districts.
* Residential neighborhoods.
* Industrial districts.
* Government locations.
* Parks.
* Highways.
* Apartment complexes.
* Businesses.
* Police facilities.

The player should generally be free to travel outside their assigned jurisdiction, but their authority, responsibilities, available calls, and department response may change.

## Events continue existing

Events should not freeze until the player arrives.

Examples:

* A suspect may leave.
* A fight may end.
* A fire may spread.
* An injured person may deteriorate.
* A stolen vehicle may continue moving.
* Witnesses may leave.
* Evidence may be disturbed.
* Another officer may resolve the incident.
* A crash may cause another crash.

## Accessible locations

The long-term goal includes entering many buildings rather than keeping the entire game outside.

Possible locations include:

* Police stations.
* Homes.
* Apartments.
* Stores.
* Gas stations.
* Restaurants.
* Hospitals.
* Courthouses.
* Jails.
* Garages.
* Gyms.
* Government buildings.
* Warehouses.

Not every building needs to be fully unique. Reusable interiors can be combined with different layouts, objects, NPCs, and circumstances.

## Promotion and qualification are different

A promotion changes rank and authority.

A qualification gives access to specialized responsibilities.

An officer may be a normal patrol officer while also being qualified for a specialized team.

Example profile:

```text
Rank:
Sergeant

Current Assignment:
Patrol

Qualifications:
SWAT
Crisis Negotiator
Firearms Instructor
```

The officer works a normal patrol shift until a qualifying situation causes a specialized activation.

```text
Department
+ Rank
+ Qualifications
+ Current Assignment
+ Current Vehicle
+ Department Policy

```

* Handcuffs.

# 6. Shift System

The player should be able to decide when to begin and end a shift rather than being trapped inside a fixed mission length.

## Shift states

Possible status flow:

```text
Off Duty
→ 10-41 / Starting Shift
→ Available
→ Call Pending
→ En Route
→ On Scene
→ Busy
→ Available
→ 10-42 / Ending Shift
```

## Already implemented

The current game tracks beginning and ending shifts.

Going on duty begins a shift. Clearing calls adds progress. Ending the shift only counts as a completed shift when the player has cleared at least one call. A shift-summary popup explains whether the shift counted toward promotion progress.

The player’s HUD and radio wheel can display duty and call status.

## Long-term shift design

Future shifts may include:

* Day, evening, and night assignments.
* Different staffing levels.
* Weather changes.
* Shift briefings.
* Vehicle assignments.
* Equipment checkout.
* Supervisor instructions.
* Special details.
* Court appearances.
* Training days.
* Overtime.
* Callouts while off duty.

The world should use faster in-game time, but it should not require hunger, sleep, thirst, or stress-maintenance meters.

# 7. Dispatch and Call System

## Design goal

Dispatch should eventually respond to activity already occurring in the world instead of only spawning traditional missions.

Example:

1. A crash occurs.
2. A witness calls 911.
3. Dispatch creates an incident.
4. The nearest available unit is assigned.
5. The event continues while units respond.

The player may also witness an event before dispatch receives it.

## Already implemented

The dispatch system currently uses structured call data capable of storing:

* Call type.
* Priority.
* Location.
* Objectives.
* Status.
* Notes.
* Resolution information.
* MDT details.

Calls are delayed after the officer becomes available rather than being assigned immediately. Additional calls can be assigned after previous calls are cleared.

The system currently supports at least two test calls:

* Suspicious person at a gas station.
* Noise complaint at an apartment building.

Call areas and subjects only respond when they match the active dispatch location.

The suspicious-person call currently requires:

* Reporting on scene.
* Contacting the subject.
* Clearing the call.

Subject interactions can create different radio messages and MDT-resolution notes depending on the call type.

## Future call categories

Possible calls include:

* Suspicious person.
* Noise complaint.
* Domestic disturbance.
* Traffic crash.
* Disabled vehicle.
* Reckless driver.
* Shoplifting.
* Theft.
* Burglary.
* Trespassing.
* Missing person.
* Welfare check.
* Mental-health crisis.
* Assault.
* Robbery.
* Shots fired.
* Barricaded suspect.
* Pursuit.
* Medical emergency.
* Fire assistance.
* Officer needs assistance.
* Civil dispute.
* Animal complaint.
* Alarm activation.

Calls should be created from combinations of reusable systems rather than each being an entirely separate scripted mission.

# 8. Interaction System Design

The interaction system has been designed conceptually but is not yet fully implemented.

## Main interaction categories

A contextual interaction wheel may use categories such as:

* Talk.
* Command.
* Investigate.
* Enforcement.
* Assistance.
* Equipment.
* Special.
* End Interaction.

The main wheel should stay consistent while the submenu changes based on circumstances.

## Circumstance-based actions

The system should read tags such as:

```text
Person in vehicle
Person injured
Weapon visible
Reasonable suspicion
Probable cause
Person detained
Person hostile
Witness present
Active crime
Medical emergency
```

A traffic stop may offer:

* Introduce yourself.
* Request license.
* Request registration.
* Explain reason for stop.
* Ask about alcohol.
* Order driver out.
* Issue warning.
* Issue citation.
* Request backup.
* End stop.

A domestic call may offer:

* Separate involved people.
* Ask what happened.
* Check for injuries.
* Ask about weapons.
* Speak to witnesses.
* Order someone to leave.
* Detain.
* Request EMS.
* Request supervisor.

## Persistent interaction posture

The officer may have a current interaction posture:

* Calm.
* Professional.
* Firm.
* Aggressive.

The player should not have to choose a tone before every sentence. The current posture influences dialogue, animation, NPC reaction, escalation, and available phrasing.

## Illegal actions

Illegal or unjustified actions should often remain selectable.

Example warning:

```text
SEARCH PERSON

No legal justification currently established.
```

The game informs the player without automatically preventing the action.

# 10. Equipment and Inventory Design

## Core rule

The player should not carry every weapon and tool at once.

Equipment availability depends on role, department, qualification, assignment, vehicle, and policy.

## Eight-slot equipment wheel

The game currently has a fixed eight-slot utility/equipment wheel.

Current behavior:

* Eight permanent sections.
* Slot one is Hands.
* Empty sections remain visible but disabled.
* Equipment positions do not shift when something is added or removed.
* The wheel is smaller and partially transparent.
* The player can continue moving and sprinting while the wheel is open.
* Equipment is selected with the mouse.
* The wheel supports tap-to-holster behavior.

## Equipment database currently present in code

The controller currently includes entries for:

* Hands.
* Flashlight.
* Taser.
* Handgun.
* Handcuffs.
* Baton.
* Pepper spray.
* Patrol rifle.
* Road spikes.
* Medical kit.
* Breaching tool.

These entries do not mean every item is fully modeled or functional. Most are currently equipment-system placeholders.

The flashlight is the first item receiving a visible first-person implementation.

## Storage design

Large or specialized equipment should remain in:

* Patrol vehicle.
* Trunk.
* Station armory.
* SWAT vehicle.
* Command truck.
* Equipment locker.

The officer must retrieve it rather than instantly selecting it from an unlimited inventory.

# 11. Injury and Medical System Design

## No traditional health bar

The long-term rule is:

> **The player does not simply lose health points. The officer receives injuries.**

Possible condition levels:

* Fine.
* Bruised.
* Wounded.
* Seriously injured.
* Critical.

## Injury effects

### Leg injury

* Limping.
* Reduced walking speed.
* Unable to sprint.
* Slower climbing.
* Reduced stability while moving.

### Arm injury

* Slower reload.
* Increased weapon sway.
* Difficulty holding equipment steady.
* Reduced melee ability.

### Chest injury

* Reduced stamina.
* Heavy breathing.
* Limited sprinting.
* Increased camera movement.

### Head injury

* Ringing ears.
* Blurred vision.
* Disorientation.
* Delayed interaction.
* Possible blackouts.

## Adrenaline

Immediately after an injury, adrenaline may temporarily reduce the symptoms.

The officer may continue moving and fighting briefly before:

* Slowing down.
* Beginning to limp.
* Breathing heavily.
* Losing aim stability.
* Realizing the injury is serious.

## EMS treatment

The current chosen gameplay idea is that the officer is not fixed automatically.

The player requests EMS. EMS arrives and visibly assesses the officer. After the assessment:

* Minor injuries may be treated on scene.
* Serious injuries may trigger a recommendation to end the shift.
* Critical injuries may make the officer unfit for duty.
* The player may sometimes refuse treatment, creating additional risk.

The simple early implementation may use an EMS assessment animation followed by restoring the player’s condition.

## Long-term possibility

More advanced versions may include:

* Injuries lasting between shifts.
* Light duty.
* Medical leave.
* Permanent limitations.
* Medical retirement.
* Injury reports.

This is a design goal and is not currently implemented.

# 12. Vehicle and Crash Consequences

Vehicle damage should not only be a visual health bar.

Possible results include:

* Repair costs.
* Department review.
* Insurance claims.
* Civilian claims.
* Supervisor response.
* Disabled patrol vehicle.
* Replacement vehicle request.
* Tow request.
* Crash report.
* Injury investigation.

A proposed rule is that the department covers a percentage of damage based on policy and fault, while the player may be responsible for the remaining cost.

The player should not be physically forced to stop and report a crash, but witnesses, cameras, vehicle telemetry, supervisors, insurance, and Internal Affairs may later reveal what happened.

This system is planned and not yet implemented.

# 15. Flashlight and First-Person Equipment — Current Status

## Flashlight beam

A functioning SpotLight3D flashlight beam is attached to the player camera.

The beam:

* Points with the camera.
* Has shadows enabled.
* Uses a defined range and cone angle.
* Becomes visible when the flashlight is equipped.

## Flashlight model

A basic physical flashlight model exists with:

* Body.
* Head.
* Lens.
* Switch.
* Metallic dark material.
* Emissive lens.

## Original hand-socket test

The flashlight was successfully attached to the character’s real right-hand bone using a `BoneAttachment3D`.

That proved:

* The skeleton hand attachment works.
* Equipment can follow character bones.
* The same attachment system can later support third-person visuals and shadows.

However, the normal walking animation caused the real hand and flashlight to swing near the waist.

## Failed experimental arm modifier

An experimental `SkeletonModifier3D` was tested to rotate the full-body shoulder, forearm, and hand into a flashlight pose.

Problems included:

* The arm twisting.
* The arm moving behind the player.
* The arm disappearing from view.
* Bone directions differing from assumed coordinates.
* Walking animation continuing to fight the pose.

That approach was abandoned for the main first-person display.

An experimental `equipment_pose_modifier.gd` file currently exists in the repository from this test, but it should be treated as unused prototype code unless later redesigned.

## Current first-person rig

The latest working setup uses a camera-attached `FirstPersonRig`.

It currently contains:

* Basic flashlight model.
* Temporary capsule-shaped forearm.
* Temporary sphere-shaped hand.
* Skin-colored temporary materials.
* Camera-relative positioning.
* Visibility controlled by equipment selection.

The old flashlight was removed from the moving full-body hand, and the camera rig now appears when the flashlight is selected.

The current scene structure is approximately:

```text
Player
└── CameraPivot
    └── PlayerCamera
        ├── InteractionRay
        ├── Flashlight
        └── FirstPersonRig
            ├── HeldFlashlight
            ├── RightForearm
            └── RightHand
```

## Current limitation

The temporary capsule forearm and sphere hand are placeholders.

The immediate next character task is replacing those shapes with a proper skinned first-person arm and hand model that matches the full character’s:

* Skin tone.
* Uniform sleeve.
* Body proportions.
* Gloves, when applicable.
* Equipment animations.

# 25. Official DUTYRA Design Rules

These rules summarize the game’s direction.

## Rule 1

> The player is not forced to make the correct choice. The world reacts to the choice.

## Rule 2

> Circumstances decide which actions are relevant, not which actions are possible.

## Rule 3

> Show a small number of useful actions while preserving a path to broader actions.

## Rule 4

> One officer experiences one continuing career.

## Rule 5

> Promotions change authority; qualifications unlock specialized opportunities.

## Rule 6

> Equipment depends on department, assignment, qualification, vehicle, and policy.

## Rule 7

> Specialized teams are part of the officer’s career, not separate game modes.

## Rule 8

> The player has injuries, not merely a visible health percentage.

## Rule 9

> Consequences should appear naturally through the world rather than arcade-style point deductions.

## Rule 10

> The UI should resemble police equipment and government software, not a flashy fantasy HUD.

## Rule 11

> Calls create circumstances rather than rigid mission scripts.

## Rule 12

> Events continue whether or not the player responds correctly or arrives on time.

## Rule 13

> Systems should be reusable so many outcomes can be created without building millions of separate mechanics.

## Rule 14

> Physical actions use direct controls; decision-heavy actions use contextual interfaces.

## Rule 15

> The full player character must feel finished before major new gameplay systems are layered onto it.
